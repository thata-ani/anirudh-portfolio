The immediate value is feedback. The longer-term value is a sharper sense of which feedback matters — and the confidence to accept, challenge, or ignore it.

The intent

I wasn't trying to build an AI that designs for you.

Not another system that says "this is good" or "change this." I wanted another perspective — one that makes you stop and ask:

DesignBesti

Why?

Who could struggle with this?

What happens outside the happy path?

Is this actually a usability problem?

How would I explain this decision to a stakeholder?

The designer still owns the decision.

The opportunity

I looked at how existing tools approached the feedback problem.

  • Human design critique

    Strong context, judgment and experience.

    Not always available when you need it.

  • User testing

    Real user behaviour.

    Requires users and a testing setup.

  • Figma-based tools

    Useful design/component checks.

    Often tied closely to the artifact.

  • Attention analysis

    Predicts visual attention.

    Answers a narrower question.

  • DesignBesti

    Structured AI challenge — another perspective, available during the design process.

    Earlier, and more often.

I wasn't trying to replace these. I saw an opportunity between them — what happens if structured critique becomes available earlier, and more often?

    What I learned

    The biggest lesson wasn't about AI. It was about feedback.

    Good feedback isn't a person or an AI telling you whether something is good or bad. Good feedback hands you a better question to think about.

    • AI is strongest when it challenges, not when it answers.
    • The hardest product decision was what not to automate.
    • Voice design matters as much as visual design.
    • A tool that makes designers think is harder to build than one that thinks for them.

    DesignBesti is an experiment in how AI can strengthen a designer's thinking without taking the thinking away.

    The AI provides another perspective. The designer decides. The designer keeps improving.

    The thinking that shaped the product.

    Same pattern every time: an observation, a question, a decision, and the experience it became.

    ObservationFeedback that isn't located is easy to nod at and hard to fix.

    QuestionWhat am I missing, and exactly where?

    DecisionScore the design across usability, accessibility, visual design, hierarchy and cognitive load — then pin each issue to a zone.

    ExperienceAnalysis — issues, wins and priority fixes, each tied to a place on the screen.

    ObservationWe test our own designs with our own habits.

    QuestionWho breaks this — and why?

    DecisionRead one screen through many users: first-time, power, accessibility, older, distracted, mobile, non-native.

    ExperienceStress Test — a discipline of changing perspective, not a longer report.

    ObservationA good UX point dies if it can't enter the product conversation.

    QuestionHow do I connect a UX observation to the business?

    DecisionSpeak the room's language: summary, risk, priority, next steps.

    ExperienceStakeholder Translator — executive summary, business risks, a priority matrix, and sprint-ready actions.

    UX observation: "Users abandon the checkout form at the address step — the form shows all 12 fields at once with no indication of progress."

    Stakeholder Translator

    Executive summary

    Checkout completion drops at the address step. The 12-field form overwhelms users — a stepped layout with progress indication would reduce abandonment.

    Business risks

    Each abandoned checkout is lost revenue. Mobile users are 3x more likely to leave a long-scroll form. Competitors with simpler flows capture the drop-off.

    Priority matrix

    High impact · Low effort

    Sprint-ready actions

    1. Break into 3 steps (contact → address → payment). 2. Add progress bar. 3. Auto-save between steps. 4. A/B test stepped vs. single-page.

    Help designers communicate decisions — not just defend interfaces.

    The shift: from "review my design" to "help me think before I design."

    ObservationThe critique people remember is the one that made them feel something.

    QuestionCan critique be memorable without losing substance?

    DecisionKeep the tone playful; keep the UX reasoning real.

    ExperienceRoast — sharp on the surface, useful underneath.

    Roast

    "Three buttons, all shouting. Pick who's in charge."

    Competing primary actions raise decision cost and blur the main path. One action should lead — the rest should step back.

    "This modal has a modal. Inception, but for bad UX."

    Stacked modals break spatial context. The user loses track of where they are and how to get back. Flatten the flow or use inline expansion.

    "Your empty state says 'No data.' Inspiring."

    Empty states are onboarding moments. Tell the user what goes here, why it matters, and what to do next — not that nothing exists yet.

    Click a roast to see the reasoning underneath.

    Playful tone. Real critique. Not a gimmick.

    ObservationA single-voice review creates blind spots — the reviewer's own biases go unchecked.

    QuestionWhat if the feedback argued with itself before reaching the designer?

    DecisionTwo AI designers. One defends the design. One challenges it. The designer watches the argument unfold.

    ExperienceDebate — structured conflict that surfaces what a single review hides.

    Debate

    Defender

    The visual hierarchy works — the primary action is larger, bolder, and positioned where the eye lands after scanning the form. Users will find it.

    Challenger

    Find it, maybe. But "Save draft" sits right next to "Submit" at the same font size. Under time pressure, a user will hit the wrong one and lose their work.

    Defender

    The color differentiation is there — filled vs. outlined. That's a standard pattern.

    Challenger

    Standard for designers. Not for a stressed user on a phone in sunlight. The contrast between those two buttons drops below usable at that size.

    Winner

    Challenger. The proximity and size similarity of destructive and constructive actions creates a real error risk. Separate them spatially or increase differentiation.

    The AI was a design problem too

    The rulebook says

    WCAG 1.4.3

    "Violates contrast ratio below 4.5:1."

    What the designer hears: compliance checklist.

    DesignBesti says

    "A user reading this in sunlight will lose the secondary text. Push the contrast so it survives a bright screen."

    What the designer hears: a reason to care.
