Intent-First Interaction

An exploration that started with voice — and turned into a question about how people should enter a product at all.

Role
Product Designer — self-initiated exploration
Year
2023
Where I explored it
Inside Cropwise Grower, the product I was designing at the time
Scope
Interaction model, entry-point concept, flow exploration

Scroll — this is how I thought about it

01 — The question

Every product asks you to learn it first.

Menus, tabs, sections, hierarchies — a map of where things live. As designers we spend our time making that map clearer: better labels, tighter grouping, fewer levels. What we rarely question is the map itself.

Navigation quietly assumes the person already knows two things — what they are looking for, and where someone else decided to file it. Most people arrive with neither. They arrive with a need, not an address.

Instead of the product asking

“Where should I go?”

It could start with

“What do you need?”

One of those puts the work on the person. The other puts it on the product. That is the whole idea — everything after this is me trying to see what it would look like.

Where should I go?

What if the user could just ask?

Not a new feature. A different front door to the one that already exists.

02 — The exploration · 2023

I explored a voice-first entry point.

Not another product. Not another service. A different way to enter the one that already existed — so that the first thing a person met wasn't a screen full of options, but a prompt that simply asked what they needed.

I was designing Cropwise Grower at the time, so that is where I tested the thought. But the idea was never about that product, or that industry. It was about the doorway — and every product has one.

Voice was simply the most direct way to make the point. Speaking is what people already do when they don't know where to look.

04 — The shift

From navigation to intent.

Before

  • User
  • Navigation
  • Feature
  • Task

The person carries the burden of translation — turning what they want into where it might be.

Exploring

  • User
  • Intent
  • Understanding
  • Relevant experience

The product carries it instead — and the person just says what they came for.

Voice was the interface. Intent was the idea.

That distinction mattered more than I understood at the time. Had I filed this away as “a voice feature”, it would have expired the moment voice fell out of fashion. Because I held on to it as an entry model, it outlived the medium.

05 — Then the world caught up

Two years later, the interaction model went mainstream.

  1. 2023

    Voice-first exploration

    “Tell the product what you need.”

  2. 2025

    Conversational AI

    “Tell the system what you want.”

Conversational interaction became familiar to a far wider audience — not because the idea was new to designers, but because the technology finally became good enough to trust with it.

The interesting part wasn't the technology. It was the interaction model.

People stopped being asked to learn a structure, and started being asked what they wanted.

06 — The connection

I wasn't trying to predict anything.

I want to be precise here, because this is the easy place to overclaim. I was not forecasting conversational AI. In 2023 nothing available to me could interpret intent reliably enough to build on — which is exactly why this stayed an exploration rather than a roadmap item.

I was questioning something much simpler:

Why should users have to understand the product before the product can understand them?

Traditional

Where do I go?

  1. Find the feature
  2. Use it

Intent-first

What do I want?

  1. Tell the product
  2. Let it determine the path

07 — The idea evolved

The interface keeps changing. The principle doesn't.

  1. 2023 Voice
  2. 2025+ Conversational AI
  3. Today Voice · Text · Vision · Multimodal

Voice was never the point. It was the medium available to me when I had the thought. The same idea now arrives as text, as vision, as multimodal input — and it will arrive as something else next.

Start with intent.

What stays constant is the direction of the effort: the product works to understand the person, rather than the person working to understand the product.

08 — What I took from it

A good idea doesn't always begin with a roadmap.

Sometimes it begins with questioning something everyone has accepted as normal. For me, that was navigation — the one thing every product has, and almost nobody argues about.

This exploration never shipped, and that's alright; not every idea needs to. What it left me with was a habit. When a product feels hard to use, I now check two things — whether the labelling is wrong, and whether the person should have been doing the finding at all.

  • Don't make the user find where the answer lives.
  • Start with what they want to accomplish.
  • The medium will change. The direction of the effort shouldn't.

Accepted normal → Question it → Model the alternative → Keep the principle → Apply it to whatever comes next.

From navigation-first to intent-first.
